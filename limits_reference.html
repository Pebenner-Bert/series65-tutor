💵 Pre-Tax vs. Post-Tax Dollars

The same dollar limit often applies to both a pre-tax and a post-tax version of the same plan (traditional vs. Roth) — the exam loves testing WHEN the tax bite happens, not just how much you can put in.
Account / PlanContribution (money going in)GrowthQualified withdrawal
Traditional 401(k)/403(b)/457(b)/TSP, Traditional IRA, SIMPLE, SEPPre-taxTax-deferredFully taxable as ordinary income
Roth 401(k)/403(b)/457(b)/TSP, Roth IRAPost-tax (after-tax dollars in)Tax-freeTax-free (5-year rule + age 59½, etc.)
HSAPre-tax (or above-the-line deduction if made outside payroll)Tax-freeTax-free — but ONLY if used for qualified medical expenses
Health FSA / Dependent Care FSAPre-tax (payroll salary reduction)N/A — use-it-or-lose-it, no investment growthTax-free for qualified expenses
529 PlanPost-tax (after-tax dollars — no federal deduction, though many states offer one)Tax-freeTax-free for qualified education expenses
Coverdell ESAPost-tax (after-tax dollars, no federal deduction)Tax-freeTax-free for qualified education expenses
Non-qualified annuityPost-tax (after-tax dollars)Tax-deferredGains taxed as ordinary income (LIFO — gains come out first); return of principal is tax-free
🧠 Memory hook: "Pay the IRS now, or pay it later — but you always pay once." Pre-tax/traditional = deduction today, ordinary income tax on withdrawal. Post-tax/Roth = no deduction today, tax-free growth AND withdrawal. The dollar limits (e.g., the $24,500 401(k) deferral, the $7,500 IRA limit) are usually a SHARED cap across the traditional and Roth version of the same plan — contributing to both doesn't double your limit.
✅ Test tip: You can ALWAYS contribute to a traditional IRA regardless of income — you might just not get to deduct it (see phase-outs below). Roth IRA contributions, by contrast, are blocked entirely once income exceeds the phase-out ceiling.

🎓 Education Savings Accounts — 529 vs. Coverdell ESA

The single most commonly tested contrast on this whole page: which one has NO federal cap, and which one is hard-capped at $2,000?
Feature529 PlanCoverdell ESA
Federal annual contribution capNone — only state aggregate limits apply$2,000 per beneficiary/year, ALL contributors combined
Indexed for inflation?N/A (no federal cap)No — fixed at $2,000 since creation
Contributor income limit?NoneYes — phases out based on contributor's MAGI (roughly $95,000–$110,000 single / $190,000–$220,000 MFJ, adjust each year)
Qualified expensesHigher ed, K-12 tuition (up to $10,000/yr), apprenticeships, student loan repayment (limited)K-12 AND higher ed expenses, broader than 529 for K-12 (books, supplies, tutoring, etc.)
Age restrictionsNoneContributions must stop at beneficiary age 18; funds generally used/rolled by age 30
Large lump-sum gifting5-year gift-tax averaging election: front-load 5 × annual exclusion ($95,000 single / $190,000 with gift-splitting for 2026) with no gift taxNo special averaging election — ordinary annual gift tax exclusion rules apply
✅ Test tip: If a question describes an unlimited/high-dollar contribution + a special multi-year gift election → it's a 529. If it describes a hard $2,000 cap shared across contributors + an age-18 contribution cutoff → it's a Coverdell ESA.

👶 Kiddie Tax — Unearned Income Tiers (2026)

A three-tier structure on a CHILD's unearned (investment) income only — wages are unaffected.
Unearned income tier2026 tax treatment
First $1,350Tax-free — covered by the dependent standard deduction
$1,351 to $2,700Taxed at the child's own (typically lower) tax rate
Above $2,700Taxed at the parent's marginal rate — reported via Form 8615
🧠 Memory hook: Kiddie tax targets UNEARNED income only (interest, dividends, capital gains) — it exists to stop parents from shifting investments into a child's lower bracket. A child's own EARNED income (wages from a job) is taxed at the child's normal rates and is completely unaffected by these tiers. Applies to kids under 18, or full-time students under 24 without earned income exceeding half their support.
